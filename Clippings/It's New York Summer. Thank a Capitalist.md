It’s New York Summer.

Like it or not, the Empire City is the center of the universe again. The New York Knicks’ underdog NBA finals run (featuring the largest comeback in NBA Finals history) had residents buzzing for weeks, and vibes are undeniably high.

According to many, this renaissance is the work of Socialist Mayor Zohran Mamdani.

Left wing commentator Kyle Kulinski, in an X post garnering 171,500 views and thousands of likes, [credited](https://x.com/KyleKulinski/status/2075581698817814800) the mayor with pressing “the giant ‘make things better’ button.” Journalist and self-described “humanist” Charlotte Alter [suggested](https://x.com/CharlotteAlter/status/2069810252946587718) to her 65,000 X followers that Mamdani alone was responsible for restoring “a sense of vitality and community to the entire city.”

In a since-deleted post that garnered more than 14,000 likes, a Mamdani enthusiast shared a video of a beautiful evening stroll through Bryant Park, with the snarky caption: “Another terrifying day in Socialist, Communist Mamdanistan apparently!”

And, yes, that stroll through Mamdani’s New York — pictured below — looks, admittedly, lovely.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/740284b2-5144-44a8-83ad-a352b9dc6f83_675x1200.png)

As you make your way through the greenery, hand-in-hand with a pretty Hinge date, taking shelter from the summer heat under a well-pruned tree in a public park, you might pause and think, *huh… if things can be this nice, that socialist stuff can’t be so bad…*

However.

You and Sarah from comms at Deloitte are nestled in expertly groomed enclaves of free market capitalism.

Indeed, a great many of New York City’s “public” cultural touchstones are maintained, in large part, thanks to its private sector. These private investments in public spaces were not compulsory, nor extracted at gunpoint. The aforementioned Bryant Park, Central Park, Madison Square Park, the Battery, and basically every other high-profile park you can name are managed and funded, in large part, by dedicated non-government “conservancies.” And behind those conservancies are, typically, a few very rich people who want their local parks to be livable (and beautiful).

Let’s start with Bryant Park, which the poster above trotted out as an example of socialism’s benefits.

The Bryant Park Corporation was established in 1980 by then-Time Inc. CEO Andrew Heiskell and urban redeveloper Daniel Biederman, with support from the Rockefeller Brothers Fund. Its founding team set out to restore the historic park after a severe downturn in the 1970s under public management; the space had become colloquially known as “Needle Park” for being a heroin shooting hotspot, and was the site of approximately 150 robberies and 10 rapes a year.

Now, with the corporation singlehandedly managing park security, sanitation, greenery maintenance, and free public programming, it is locally beloved for its cleanliness and safety, and draws more than 12 million annual visitors. Its current board of directors includes New York real estate royalty, a famous fashion designer, and Michael Fuchs, the Chairman of HBO.

Little Island, a manmade island built on stilts over the Hudson River, was dreamed up by Barry Diller, the billionaire co-founder of Fox and chairman of IAC and Expedia; his philanthropy fully funds and operates it today. Rockefeller Center’s plaza and ice rink is entirely corporately owned and operated, with the real estate firm Tishman Speyer managing and maintaining the complex. Fort Tryon Park was funded and designed by American Industrialist John D. Rockefeller, before he donated it to the city under private management of the Fort Tryon Park Trust.

In 1986, a young real estate developer named Donald Trump took over the city government’s long-delayed, disastrous Central Park Ice Skating Rink project, and resurrected the dilapidated rink in just four months and $750,000 under budget. Wollman Rink was entirely funded and managed by the Trump Organization until 2021, when the city government terminated their contract.

The rink was just one feature of Central Park’s private sector revival. In the 70s and 80s, Central Park was coated in 50,000 square feet of graffiti, its benches and lamps were left broken, and theft and crime were rampant. Then, a group of concerned New Yorkers (including philanthropist and businessman William Sperry Beinecke) founded the private Central Park Conservancy, whose board has historically included wealthy financiers such as John Paulson — who gave $100 million to the park in 2012.

The conservancy now raises more than 75% of Central Park’s annual operating budget and has overseen over $1 billion invested into the park. It also manages all facility maintenance, landscaping, and cleanliness, and funds a $50 million “Ranger Corps” to aid the NYPD in public safety efforts.

Beyond the parks, even the Hudson River, once black with oil sludge, was cleaned up beginning in the 80s in response to lawsuits from RFK Jr’s Hudson Riverkeeper, a private organization of fishermen and attorneys. The Riverkeepers helped set a precedent for decades of EPA and DOJ prosecution.

And don’t forget that Citi Bikes, Mamdani’s [transportation method](https://time.com/7330318/zohran-mamdani-citi-bike-mayor/) of choice, are fully funded through corporate sponsorship, without a dime of taxpayer money involved.

Almost everything in a New York City brochure, every condescending dinner party story told by college grads back home in Indiana for Thanksgiving, hinges upon the competence and benevolence of New York’s unparalleled capitalist class.

The few exceptions are disastrous.

Washington Square Park, which has been plagued by homelessness and crime for decades, is almost entirely managed and funded by the city government’s parks department. Its small, recently-ratified conservancy is confined to supplying 17 additional park upkeep jobs, and little else — thanks to the Washington Square Park “Private Conservancy Watch,” a group of local residents who’ve fought tooth and nail to resist private sector management at all costs. (The community board is now debating whether to [install gates](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Px3uQ0wu3HI) outside the park to curb persistent late-night disorder.)

Since 2018, the government-managed East River Park has been subject to a massive flood-prevention project, entirely run and funded by city agencies.

The starting budget $335 million, which was incrementally expanded to $1.45 billion by 2018. The Mayor’s Office justified the budget raise by citing an expert-Lee impact study that required the change. However, Freedom of Information requests proved that no such study existed.

The project took two years to break ground, keeps getting delayed, and will likely exceed its absurdly bloated budget upon completion.

As much as the NYU polisci sophomores may hate NYC’s hardworking business owners, they are the center of gravity around which eight million people orbit. And although Mayor Mamdani sponsored several public-funded outdoor watch parties for the 2026 NBA finals, it was privately-funded screens outside bars, shops, bodegas, and restaurants that New Yorkers flocked to when they watched their favorite millionaires win it all.

New York’s post-COVID bounceback is a testament to the enduring strength of our entrepreneurs and the unrelenting progress innate to capitalism. It is the result of millions of private, independent success stories. It is *not* validation that socialist policies actually work.

Should we forget that, and instead heap undue praise onto insurgent collectivist forces that hate our brightest minds, we will cede the city to ruins.

For now, the greatness and beauty of New York persists, and does so wholly in spite of incessant government overreach, not because of it.
